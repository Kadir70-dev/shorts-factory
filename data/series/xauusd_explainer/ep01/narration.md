# XAU/USD Explained — Ep. 1 narration

**s1** [00 · hook · stock]  
Gold has been money, a war chest, a crisis hedge, and one of the most closely watched prices on Earth.

**s2** [00 · hook · mgfx]  
But when traders say XAU/USD is moving — what are they actually looking at?

**s3** [00 · context · chart]  
XAU/USD is simply the price of one troy ounce of gold, quoted in US dollars.

**s4** [00 · evidence · mgfx]  
One number. And underneath it — interest rates, currency strength, inflation, and global fear, all priced in at once.

**s5** [01 · context · stock]  
XAU is gold's currency code — the 'X' marks it as a commodity, the same way EUR marks the euro or JPY marks the yen.

**s6** [01 · reveal · mgfx]  
So XAU/USD isn't a stock ticker. It's an exchange rate — gold, priced as if it were a currency of its own.

**s7** [02 · context · archival]  
It's quoted in dollars because the dollar is still the world's reserve currency — the common language most global prices get translated through.

**s8** [02 · evidence · stock]  
Oil, gold, most international trade — priced in dollars first, then converted into whatever currency you actually spend.

**s9** [02 · context · mgfx]  
That's a structural fact of the current monetary system — not a comment on any single country's economy.

**s10** [03 · mechanism · chart]  
So if XAU/USD reads two thousand, that means one troy ounce of gold costs two thousand US dollars. Nothing more complicated than that.

**s11** [03 · mechanism · mgfx]  
The number rises, gold got more expensive in dollars. It falls, gold got cheaper — or the dollar simply got stronger.

**s12** [04 · question · mgfx]  
So what actually moves that number? It's rarely one thing. It's usually four.

**s13** [05 · mechanism · stock]  
First: the dollar itself. Gold is priced in dollars, so when the dollar weakens against other currencies, gold usually costs more in dollar terms — even if nothing about gold changed.

**s14** [05 · evidence · chart]  
A weaker dollar buys less of almost everything priced in dollars. Gold is no exception.

**s15** [06 · context · archival]  
Second: what the Federal Reserve does with interest rates.

**s16** [06 · mechanism · mgfx]  
Interest rates change how attractive it is to hold cash and bonds instead of an asset that pays you nothing to hold it.

**s17** [07 · reveal · mgfx]  
Here's the concept that actually drives it: real yield. Take the interest rate. Subtract expected inflation. What's left is the real yield.

**s18** [07 · mechanism · mgfx]  
Traders don't watch the interest rate on its own. They watch it against inflation expectations — because that gap is what actually matters to an asset like gold that pays no yield at all.

**s19** [07 · mechanism · chart]  
When real yields are high, bonds beat gold easily. When real yields fall toward zero — or below it — gold's zero yield stops being a disadvantage.

**s20** [07 · payoff · mgfx]  
That's the opportunity cost of holding gold — what you give up by not earning interest — rising and falling with real rates.

**s21** [08 · context · stock]  
Third: inflation expectations. Gold has a long reputation as a store of value when a currency's purchasing power looks likely to erode.

**s22** [08 · mechanism · mgfx]  
Traders don't just react to today's inflation number. They react to where they expect inflation to go next.

**s23** [09 · tension · stock]  
Fourth: fear. When markets get nervous — a banking scare, a war, a shock nobody priced in — investors often rotate into gold.

**s24** [09 · reveal · threejs]  
Not because gold is exciting. Because its value isn't tied to any single government's promise.

**s25** [10 · evidence · chart]  
That same logic applies to central banks. They've bought more than a thousand tonnes of gold a year, three years running.

**s26** [10 · evidence · chart]  
That's more than double the average pace from the decade before it.

**s27** [10 · payoff · chart]  
Gold recently passed the euro to become the world's second-largest reserve asset — behind only the US dollar itself.

**s28** [11 · tension · mgfx]  
Here's what trips up a lot of beginners: gold doesn't always do what the textbook says it should.

**s29** [11 · evidence · stock]  
Rates can rise and gold can still rise too — if inflation expectations are climbing even faster, or fear is doing more work than rates that week.

**s30** [11 · mechanism · mgfx]  
Gold isn't reacting to one input. It's pricing all four at once — and they don't always agree.

**s31** [12 · tension · stock]  
Now, XAU/USD isn't just a price to understand — for some, it's also something to trade. That's where people get into trouble.

**s32** [12 · mechanism · chart]  
In the US, federal law has restricted leveraged retail gold trading since 2010 — these contracts must either deliver real metal within twenty-eight days, or trade on a regulated exchange.

**s33** [12 · consequence · mgfx]  
The CFTC has taken multi-million dollar enforcement action against offshore platforms offering exactly this kind of leveraged gold trading.

**s34** [12 · payoff · mgfx]  
That doesn't mean gold can't be traded responsibly. It means leverage on a volatile asset is exactly as risky as it sounds.

**s35** [13 · turn · mgfx]  
Here's the honest distinction: understanding why gold moves is not the same as predicting where it moves next.

**s36** [13 · consequence · stock]  
Professionals who study this for a living still get the short term wrong constantly. The mechanics are knowable. The next candle isn't.

**s37** [14 · payoff · stock]  
So — XAU/USD isn't mysterious. It's the dollar, interest rates, inflation, fear, and global demand, compressed into a single number.

**s38** [14 · payoff · chart]  
One troy ounce. Priced in dollars. Moved by four forces most people never learn to see.

**s39** [14 · cta · mgfx]
