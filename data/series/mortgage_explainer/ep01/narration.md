# Mortgage Explained — Ep. 1 narration

Total estimated runtime: 228.1s (~3.80 min)

**s1** [00 · hook · stock]  
A five hundred thousand dollar house doesn't cost five hundred thousand dollars. Finance it for thirty years, and the number that actually leaves your pocket can look completely different.

**s2** [01 · context · character]  
Meet John. John found a home he loves — listed at five hundred thousand dollars.

**s3** [01 · context · mgfx]  
Almost nobody pays that in cash. Most buyers borrow the difference between what they put down, and what the home actually costs.

**s4** [02 · evidence · chart]  
For this example, John puts down 10 percent — 50,000 dollars. That's his choice for this scenario; real buyers put down anywhere from a few percent to twenty percent or more.

**s5** [02 · reveal · chart]  
The rest — 450,000 dollars — is what he actually borrows. That's the mortgage principal.

**s6** [03 · mechanism · archival]  
The bank agrees to lend him that principal over thirty years, at a fixed interest rate. For this example, we'll use six and a half percent — a hypothetical rate for illustration, not a live market quote.

**s7** [03 · mechanism · mgfx]  
Fixed means the rate never changes. Whatever John's payment is in year one, it's the same in year thirty.

**s8** [04 · reveal · chart]  
Run those numbers through a standard mortgage formula, and John's principal-and-interest payment comes out to 2,844 dollars a month.

**s9** [04 · turn · voxel]  
But that single number doesn't all go to the bank as profit. Here's where a payment like that actually travels.

**s10** [05 · evidence · stock]  
Property taxes, homeowners insurance, and — because John put down less than twenty percent — private mortgage insurance, all get added on top of that principal-and-interest payment.

**s11** [05 · context · mgfx]  
Lenders usually bundle all of that into one monthly bill, called an escrow payment.

**s12** [05 · evidence · chart]  
For this illustrative example: taxes add roughly 458 dollars a month. Insurance, about 150. PMI, about 281. Together, John's real monthly payment is closer to 3,734 dollars.

**s13** [05 · payoff · mgfx]  
PMI protects the bank, not John — and by federal law, it has to come off automatically once his loan balance drops to seventy-eight percent of the home's original value.

**s14** [06 · tension · mgfx]  
Here's the part almost nobody explains well: not every one of John's payments works the same way.

**s15** [06 · evidence · chart]  
In year one, 85 percent of every payment John makes is interest. Only about 15 percent actually reduces what he owes.

**s16** [06 · evidence · chart]  
By year fifteen, that's shifted — 62 percent interest, 38 percent principal.

**s17** [06 · reveal · chart]  
By year thirty, it's flipped almost completely: less than one percent interest, the rest is pure principal.

**s18** [06 · payoff · mgfx]  
That's amortization: the bank collects most of its interest early, while John owes the most money — and less interest later, as the balance shrinks.

**s19** [06 · mechanism · mgfx]  
Here's why: interest is calculated on whatever John still owes, not on the original loan. Early on, he still owes almost all of it, so interest takes the bigger share of the payment.

**s20** [06 · mechanism · mgfx]  
As his balance drops, month after month, there's less principal left to charge interest on — so a shrinking slice goes to interest, and a growing slice chips away at what he owes.

**s21** [07 · reveal · chart]  
Add up every interest payment over all thirty years, and here's the number that surprises most buyers: 573,950 dollars in interest alone.

**s22** [07 · consequence · chart]  
That's more than John originally borrowed. Borrow 450,000, and — on this schedule — he ends up paying 1,023,950 dollars total.

**s23** [08 · evidence · chart]  
And that total is extremely sensitive to the rate. The exact same loan, at 5.5 percent instead of 6.5, costs about 104,132 dollars less in interest over the life of the loan.

**s24** [08 · payoff · mgfx]  
Go the other way — 7.5 percent instead of 6.5 — and it costs about 108,777 dollars more. Same house, same amount borrowed, a very different outcome — just from the rate.

**s25** [09 · question · character]  
Now here's a choice that's actually in John's control: what if he pays an extra three hundred dollars toward principal, every single month?

**s26** [09 · reveal · chart]  
On this schedule, that alone pays off the loan about 7 years early — and saves him over 155,173 dollars in interest.

**s27** [09 · consequence · mgfx]  
It isn't free money — it's real cash out of his budget every month, and it isn't automatically the best move for everyone. But it shows how much leverage extra principal has, especially early in a loan.

**s28** [10 · payoff · stock]  
Which is really the point: the sticker price was never the full story. What actually matters is the rate John gets, how long he finances it, and what he chooses to do with his payment every single month.

**s29** [10 · payoff · mgfx]  
A five hundred thousand dollar house can mean very different total costs for two different buyers, on two different mortgages.

**s30** [10 · consequence · mgfx]  
And the sticker price still isn't the whole picture — closing costs, ongoing maintenance, and how those escrow items change over time all shape what a home really costs to own, well beyond the number on the listing.

**s31** [11 · cta · mgfx]  
This has been one hypothetical example — not a prediction for your mortgage, your rate, or your local taxes. A real lender can run your actual numbers.
